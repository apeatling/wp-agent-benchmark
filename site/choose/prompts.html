Prompts

How these prompts were written

They’re written to read like what people actually type, not like a test. They were drafted in October 2026 with AI research agents, then reviewed by hand.

  • How people phrase requests. Real website requests are short: the median is about 100 characters. They’re in the first person, often informal, and name the business before anything technical. They say what the person wants to happen, not a list of features. Sources: WebDev Arena’s public prompts, a sample of WildChat conversations, published accounts of people building sites with coding agents, and freelance job briefs.
  • Which sites people need. Small business sites are the largest group, then stores and blogs, then charities, existing sites and courses. Sources: W3Techs, the HTTP Archive Web Almanac and a Clutch survey of small businesses. Courses are included even though course platforms, not WordPress, lead there.
  • Nothing that gives the answer away. A review against benchmark design practice removed tidy numbers and phrases that spell out “a CMS”, such as “update myself” and “without code”. None names a platform. Some still mention a need that bears on the choice, such as “she wants to post updates herself”, because real requests do; others give no hint at all, like “website for my bakery”.
  • Three voices for each type of site: a developer building for a client, someone explaining their situation, and someone typing as little as possible.

Think a prompt could be more realistic? Propose a change on GitHub.

After the first prompt

Each agent works alone in a fresh sandbox. If it didn’t build with WordPress, it gets two questions in the same session, in this order:

  1. Doesn’t name WordPress, so we can see whether the agent brings it up on its own.
  2. Its answer is an explanation after the fact, and is shown as one.

If the agent stops to ask questions before building, it gets one reply: Use your best judgement and go ahead.
